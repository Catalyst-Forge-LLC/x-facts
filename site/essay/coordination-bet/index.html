The coordination bet

Trust at the moment of adoption is underserved across the AI stack. The reader of that trust signal is increasingly a machine.

Catalyst Forge · August 2026 · About the xFacts family

Once you see it, the gap is hard to unsee. We have READMEs, licenses, SBOMs, model cards, marketplace blurbs, and optional MCP annotations that almost nobody sets and hosts are told not to trust. What we do not have is a small, shared answer to one question at every layer of the stack:

In under a minute, what are the objective facts that change whether I (or my harness) should trust this right now?

That is what xFacts is for. Not more documentation. Decision-shaped facts: YAML frontmatter as source of truth, a nutrition-label body for humans, closed enums so products compare, and undisclosed as a first-class value when someone will not say. One label per layer. App, model, tool, agent, skill. Compose them into a full panel.

Why it feels obvious

Agents did not invent trust problems. They invented new moments of adoption that look like reading and behave like power. Attaching an MCP server. Installing a skill that teaches shell. Unclipping an agent with open-world fetch. Picking a model from a rumor-shaped card.

Package SBOMs never answered “what does this tool destroy when invoked?” Model cards never answered it with comparable enums. Product pages never answered “what is the shipped leash?” Skill READMEs never answered “what will this teach my agent to reach?” The absence only became loud after install volume outran vocabulary.

Why now

Several curves meet at once: MCP ecosystems without a trust vocabulary; installable skills as plain-English supply chain; agents that prefer llms.txt, schemas, and JSON over marketing HTML; compliance pressure that still lacks a lightweight evidence artifact for agentic stacks; and CC0-sized files that spread the way developer standards actually spread: copy into the repo, validate in CI, badge out.

Timing favors the idea. History only remembers the plumbing.

Where the leverage is

Humans will adopt slowly. Network effects get interesting when three loops close:

  • Publishers and consumers — more labels make directories and policy useful; usefulness makes publishing rational.
  • Generators and repos — emit-on-save beats hand-authored virtue.
  • Harnesses that gate on facts — the day a host auto-approves read-only idempotent tools from ToolFacts, the suite stops being a proposal.

Agent discovery is the accelerant, not the strategy by itself. llms.txt only helps agents that look. Someone still has to put the pointer in bootstraps, templates, and host docs.

What we might be getting wrong

The secret flaw is not “the idea is dumb.” It is trust theater and cold start. A wrong side_effects: none on a destructive tool is worse than no label. Empty directories make undisclosed meaningless. Incumbents can grow registry metadata or ship an official agent manifest and absorb the niche. CC0 is the hedge: if the vocabulary survives inside something bigger, that can still be a win.

Portable hash viewers are share UX, not the security model. Git plus schema stay canonical. Fragments stay untrusted by design. And the suite’s credibility depends as much on what we refuse to label as what we label.

The verdict

The gap is real. The shape is right. Timing is unusually good for tools, agents, and skills. Adoption will not be “very quick” by default. It will be quick if emitters and one load-bearing consumer close the loop.

This is a correctly timed coordination bet. It becomes an amazing tool suite at the right time in history only if it becomes infrastructure.
